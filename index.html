R.01

Do LLM Fingerprints Survive Outside English?

First author · LangLLM
Under review · IEEE BigData 2026 High School Symposium (decision 9 Oct)
Multilingual NLPStylometryInterpretabilityLLM-as-Judge

Attributing text to a specific model has been studied almost only in English. We collect 840 essays from five frontier models (GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini 3.5 Flash, Grok 4.3, DeepSeek V4 Pro) in seven languages from English to Hindi, and attribute them with 21 interpretable features computed from Universal Dependencies parses. A five-way classifier reaches 59–72% in every language against 20% chance, and we detect no decline as a language's resources fall. Character n-grams are more accurate within a language, but the interpretable features transfer between languages better (49% against 41%). Attribution holds at 57–70% on machine-translated text, while the five models, asked which of them wrote each text, score only 20–25%.

R.02

Self-Attribution Is Not Self-Recognition: A Peer Baseline for LLM Judges

First author · CompLLM · with Ryan Erlikhman & Michael Tarekegn · Advisor: Philo Juang (Google DeepMind)
Under review · NeurIPS 2026 · JUDGe Workshop (decision 29 Sept)
NLPLLM-as-JudgeStylometryEvaluation

LLM-as-judge pipelines anonymize candidate responses on the assumption that a judge cannot tell who wrote what. I run five frontier models over the same 190 responses under two elicitation formats and score every self-attribution rate against a peer baseline: what the other four judges get on the same text. Claude names itself on 86.8% of its own writing while its peers identify that text at 53.9%; GPT shows a similar edge, two models are neutral, and Grok lands below the chance floor on itself. A stylometric classifier over 18 interpretable features recovers the true author at 86.3%, above every judge, so the signal is in the text — most judges just do not use it. Fully reproducible in one command, no API key.

R.03

Proximity, Not Transmission: What the Patent Record Can and Cannot Show About NLP and Surveillance

First author · Earshot · with Michael Tarekegn
Submission in progress · NeurIPS 2026 · AI for Peace Workshop (due 21 Sept)
NLPPatent CitationsDual-Use AILLM Classification

We introduce Earshot, a purpose-based audit of the patents that cite language-technology research. It links 127,851 ACL Anthology papers to the 9,048 USPTO patents citing them and classifies what those patents are for (surveillance, military, dual-use or neither) with a rubric that separates a system's operator from its subject, and authentication from identification. In a random sample of 2,374 patents with applicant names hidden, we find 11 surveillance patents (0.46%), no subfield that stands out, no trend by year, and no detectable difference from science-citing patents of the same grant era (odds ratio 1.03, 95% CI 0.38–2.78). Revealing the applicant changes 10 of 31 non-neutral labels, and 91.6% of the citations sit only on a patent's front page: citations measure proximity, not what a patent is actually for.

R.04

How Robust Are the Legatum Prosperity Index Rankings? A Monte Carlo and Sensitivity Analysis of Weighting, Normalization, and Aggregation

First author · composite-index robustness · with Ryan Erlikhman (LACES)
Accepted with revisions · Journal of High School Science · in revision
PythonRank StatisticsRobustnessReproducibility

The Legatum Prosperity Index ranks 167 countries by an equal-weighted mean of twelve pillar scores. We reproduce every published score and rank exactly, then vary the weights, normalization and aggregation: 10,000 draws under each of three weight priors and a 131,072-run Sobol' design. The ranking holds as a whole (median Spearman ≥ 0.9899) but not in mid-table: the median 90% rank interval is 22 places overall and 36 for ranks 68–100. The paper's new result is rank leverage, c²(1 − ρ²): a weight can reorder countries only through the share of its pillar's variance that the composite doesn't share, times the pillar's squared relative spread. With arithmetic aggregation it tracks the weights' total effects under all four normalizations (Spearman 0.91–0.99), and equal weights of 1/12 give leverages that differ sixfold.

R.05

Does Portfolio Optimization Beat Splitting Evenly?

Markowitz vs. the 1/N portfolio · with Ryan Erlikhman (LACES)
Accepted with revisions · Oxford Journal of Student Scholarship · in revision
Quant FinancePortfolio TheorySimulation

Modern portfolio theory says to hold assets in the proportions that maximize reward-to-risk — the Markowitz portfolio. We compare it to simply holding equal amounts of each asset (1/N) on a factor model calibrated to real market data. Across 250 simulations the 1/N portfolio earns a higher Sharpe (0.70 vs 0.51) and wins 74% of the time. Given the true covariance but estimated means, the optimizer still loses; given true means, it is nearly optimal. Estimating expected returns is the whole problem, and shrinkage only fixes it with about two decades of stationary data.

    which model does your writing read like?

    Paste any paragraph. It measures four simple style features (sentence length, lexical diversity, commas per sentence and "AI vocabulary") and shows which of six rough profiles your text sits closest to.

    reads most like—
    A toy that shows the idea, not the paper's classifier. The six profiles are rough averages set by hand, not fitted to the study's data, and the Human one isn't from the study at all. The percentages split 100% across the profiles by closeness, so they are not probabilities or accuracy. The paper itself collects 190 responses from five frontier models, and a classifier over 18 interpretable features names the true author 86.3% of the time.
