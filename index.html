Research

My research focuses on evaluating language models: identifying which model wrote a text, and testing whether LLM judges and benchmarks give reliable results. Two further papers, written with my brother Ryan, test standard quantitative methods in portfolio construction and in a global prosperity index.

Which AI wrote this, and can you still tell outside English?

Interpretable Multilingual Attribution of Frontier LLMs

Best Paper Finalist (12 of 130 accepted papers), IEEE BigData 2026 High School Symposium, Phoenix, Dec. 17. With Michael Tarekegn and Philo Juang; co-first author.

We collected 840 essays from five frontier models in seven languages. Twenty-one features a person can inspect name the right model 59–72% of the time, against 20% chance. The models themselves barely beat chance at naming the author.

Research questions
  1. Can five frontier models be told apart by their writing within each language? Yes. Twenty-one features a person can inspect name the author 59–72% of the time in all seven languages, against 20% chance, and 54–76% at a reading level the classifier never saw.
  2. Does attribution weaken in languages with less training data? No decline found down a resource ranking fixed in advance, though a drop of up to 17 points from English to Hindi can’t be ruled out.
  3. Does a model’s style carry across languages? Partly. Trained in one language and tested in another, the features reach 37–59%, and they transfer better than character n-grams (49% against 41%).
  4. Do the models write more alike in lower-resource languages? No, by the test planned in advance; the drop that does appear is mostly Grok.
  5. Does machine translation erase the signal? No. Retrained on essays run through Google Translate and MiniMax, the features still reach 57–70%.
  6. Can the models tell which of them wrote an essay? Barely. Asked directly, the five models name the right author 20.3–25.4% of the time, close to chance; only Claude does better.

How few test questions does it take to change which AI judge wins?

Curated to Win: Certifying Reward-Model and Judge Selection Against Evaluation-Set Curation

Accepted, ARRSI Workshop at AACL-IJCNLP 2026, Hengqin, China, Nov. 10. Sole author.

AI labs pick a reward model or an AI judge by its benchmark score. I showed how to certify when that pick holds. Removing 12 particular items of RewardBench’s 2,985 makes the runner-up the winner. The paper covers 306 models across RewardBench and RewardBench 2.

Research questions
  1. How much of a benchmark could be removed without changing which reward model wins? Very little. Removing 12 particular items of RewardBench’s 2,985 makes the runner-up the winner; under RewardBench’s own scoring code, 11 are enough. RewardBench 2’s leader survives the removal of any 39.
  2. Is that bound tight, or only a worst case on paper? Tight near the top. Linear programs over item weights, rounded to real item subsets, reach it on both leaderboards.
  3. How many models could a curator make the winner? Three, on RewardBench, with a curation budget of just 1% of the benchmark’s weight.
  4. Does the certificate work on a small validation set? Yes. On 200 random RewardBench items, the 8% of selections whose top two are more than 2 points apart agree with the full benchmark’s winner 87% of the time, against 43% for the rest.

Should you just split your money evenly?

Does Portfolio Optimization Beat Splitting Evenly?

Published, Oxford Journal of Student Scholarship, October 2026. With my brother, Ryan Erlikhman; co-first author.

Finance textbooks teach Markowitz optimization: choose the mix of assets with the best return for its risk. Ryan and I tested it against splitting money evenly across ten assets, in 250 simulated markets calibrated to real data, and the even split won 74% of the time.

Research questions
  1. Does textbook portfolio optimization beat splitting money evenly? No. Across 250 simulations of ten assets the even split won 74% of the time, with a Sharpe ratio of 0.70 against Markowitz’s 0.51, worth about 3 points of return a year.
  2. Why does the optimizer lose? Estimated expected returns. Given the true covariances but estimated returns, it still trails; given the true returns, it is nearly optimal.
  3. Do the standard fixes rescue it? Not with the data markets provide. Shrinkage and a no-shorting rule help as the theory predicts, but shrinkage does nothing for mean returns; the shrunk portfolio pulls ahead only with about two decades of stationary returns.

Do AI judges recognize their own writing?

Whose Line Is It Anyway? Auditing Self- and Other-Recognition in LLM Judges

Preprint, arXiv forthcoming. With Michael Tarekegn and Philo Juang; co-first author.

A judge that recognizes its own writing could favor it. We tested nine LLM judges on 1,270 texts (19,000+ judgments), with two controls the usual test leaves out: how often a judge names itself on text it didn’t write, and how often other models name it as the author. With both controls, no judge recognized its own writing better than its strongest peer did.

Research questions
  1. Does an LLM judge tell its own writing apart from other models’? Sometimes. The usual self-naming test flags 15 of 37 cases, but in 7 of them the judge names itself just as often on other models’ texts; 8 survive that false-alarm control.
  2. Is that recognition specific to the judge, or can other models spot the same texts? Never clearly specific. Only 3 cases beat the pooled peers and none beats the judge’s strongest single peer: Claude names itself on 33 of its 38 chat-app essays, and GPT names Claude on 32.
  3. Do the texts carry information the judges miss? Yes. A style classifier attributes 86% of the chat-app essays, and each open-weight judge’s own likelihood ranks its text first on 58–87% of prompts, yet those judges are near chance at naming themselves.

How fragile are the world’s prosperity rankings?

How Robust Are the Legatum Prosperity Index Rankings? A Monte Carlo and Sensitivity Analysis of Weighting, Normalization, and Aggregation

In revision, Journal of High School Science. With Ryan Erlikhman; co-first author.

The Legatum Prosperity Index ranks 167 countries by averaging twelve pillar scores with equal weights. Ryan and I rebuilt it exactly, then ran 131,072 variations of its weights and methods. The overall ranking holds, but a mid-table country’s place can swing across a range of 36.

Research questions
  1. Can the published index be rebuilt exactly? Yes. The twelve pillar scores reproduce every overall score to within 5×10−11 and every rank exactly.
  2. How stable are the rankings when the weights, normalization and aggregation change? Stable overall, not mid-table. The whole ranking keeps a median rank correlation of 0.99, but countries ranked 68 to 100 have a median 90% rank interval 36 places wide.
  3. Does each pillar’s equal 1/12 weight give it equal influence? No. Personal Freedom’s weight has the largest effect (a Sobol’ total effect of 0.23), and under equal weights the pillars’ rank leverage differs by a factor of six.
  4. What decides how far a weight can move the rankings? Its rank leverage, c2(1 − ρ2), which the paper derives; it tracks the weights’ Sobol’ effects with a Spearman correlation of 0.91 to 0.99.
